The statement "Every polynomial of degree n has n roots" is not quite correct as stated. A more precise version is:

**Theorem (Fundamental Theorem of Algebra).** Every nonconstant polynomial with complex coefficients has at least one complex root. As a consequence, every polynomial of degree n with complex coefficients can be factored into n linear factors (counting multiplicities), so it has exactly n roots in the complex plane (counting multiplicities).

Here is a proof of the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, which implies the corrected statement above.

### Proof

Let p(z) be a nonconstant polynomial with complex coefficients. We want to show that p(z) has at least one complex root.

1. **Analyticity.** The polynomial p(z) is an entire function (analytic on the whole complex plane).

2. **Behavior at infinity.** As |z| → ∞, the dominant term of p(z) is its leading term a_n z^n (a_n ≠ 0). Therefore, |p(z)| → ∞ as |z| → ∞.

3. **Minimum modulus principle.** Since p(z) is entire and |p(z)| → ∞ as |z| → ∞, the function |p(z)| attains its minimum value at some finite point z0 ∈ C. (This follows from the fact that |p(z)| is continuous and coercive.)

4. **Local behavior near z0.** Suppose, for contradiction, that p(z0) ≠ 0. Then p(z) is nonzero in a neighborhood of z0. Consider the function f(z) = 1/p(z), which is analytic in a neighborhood of z0.

5. **Maximum modulus principle.** Since f(z) is analytic and nonzero near z0, the maximum modulus principle implies that |f(z)| cannot have a local maximum at z0. Therefore, there are points z arbitrarily close to z0 where |f(z)| > |f(z0)|, i.e., |p(z)| < |p(z0)|. This contradicts the fact that z0 is a point where |p(z)| attains its minimum.

6. **Conclusion.** The contradiction shows that p(z0) = 0, so p(z) has a complex root.

### Corollary: Factorization and counting roots

Once we know that every nonconstant polynomial has a complex root, we can factor out a linear term and repeat the argument on the quotient polynomial. By induction, every polynomial of degree n can be factored as
p(z) = a_n (z − r1)(z − r2)⋯(z − rn),
where r1, r2, …, rn are complex numbers (the roots) and a_n is the leading coefficient. If a root appears multiple times in the factorization, we count it with its multiplicity. Thus, a degree-n polynomial has exactly n roots in C, counted with multiplicity.

- The theorem is false if we restrict to real numbers: a real polynomial of degree n need not have n real roots (e.g., x^2 + 1 has no real roots).

1. Closure: For all a, b in G, the result of the operation a·b is also in G.
2. Associativity: For all a, b, c in G, (a·b)·c = a·(b·c).
